SahelSense: a hybrid VTOL that senses in the air

A survey over Sahel margins needs two things at once: vertical takeoff from whatever ground is available, and the endurance of a fixed wing once it is up. This aircraft lifts on four rotors, transitions to wing-borne flight under its own logic, and computes vegetation indices onboard rather than shipping raw imagery home.

02Flight test

28 flights, including autonomous transition

The aircraft has flown 28 times. That record covers manual hover, assisted hover, wing-borne cruise, and autonomous waypoint missions in which the aircraft transitions between hover and forward flight on its own, with no pilot input at the transition. Every flight was logged, and the logs are what the propulsion work below rests on.

03Propulsion

A hover fault, traced rather than tuned

The aircraft climbed away cleanly and then could not hold altitude. It drifted sideways or sank from around a metre and a half, and throttle stopped answering above roughly half stick. It did this in QLOITER and also in QSTABILIZE, which is manual throttle with no autonomous altitude correction at all, and it kept doing it across many arms, sessions and hardware changes.

The reflex in that situation is to reach for the gains. I treated it as a measurement problem instead, and ran a progression where each step could eliminate something, rather than changing several things at once and flying again to see what happened. Several of those steps found real faults that turned out not to be the cause, which is worth saying plainly, because a fault you find is not automatically the fault you are looking for.

  1. Rule out power delivery

    All power wiring replaced from 16 AWG to 10 AWG, batteries swapped. Voltage on the monitored circuit stayed stable and the behaviour did not change. Current capacity was checked separately and was never the constraint: the 5000 mAh 20C pack flown at that stage supplies around 100 A, against a hover draw of roughly 25 to 28 A across the four lift motors.

  2. Find the battery compensation bug, and rule it out too

    Parameter analysis showed the QuadPlane was computing its thrust ceiling from the wrong battery. Q_M_BAT_IDX pointed at the monitored instance, which was the tractor motor’s pack, not the one powering the lift motors. Logs showed the ceiling clamping to between 68 and 92 percent. That is a genuine misconfiguration, and it was fixed twice over, by rewiring the power module onto the lift pack and by disabling compensation outright. The fault persisted, so it was not the cause.

  3. Separate the motors from the control loop

    With Q_ESC_CAL = 1, stick input goes straight to PWM and the stabilisation loop is bypassed. In that state the aircraft behaved correctly and predictably all the way to 1889 PWM. Set back to 0, with the loop active again, the fault returned immediately. That put the motors and ESCs in the clear on direct commands and located the problem in what happens when the controller is asking for more than it is getting.

  4. Read the logs motor by motor

    Dataflash logs were rebuilt from the telemetry files by reassembling the embedded log chunks, then parsed directly. Across six flights, spanning battery swaps, rewiring, firmware changes and a tail removal, motor 2 was the most saturated motor in every single one, spending between 13.6 and 44.8 percent of flight time commanded above 1900 PWM. Motor 3 was consistently the least loaded. An asymmetry that survives every other variable points at one arm, not at a general power problem.

  5. Recheck the thrust budget from scratch

    Late on it emerged the lift props were 13 inch, not the 16 inch the sums had assumed. That invalidated the comfortable 2:1 figure that had been steering the investigation away from thrust for weeks. On the datasheet’s nearest tested size the QM4208 makes about 1780 g at full throttle, against roughly 1150 g per motor needed to hover at the 4.6 kg the aircraft weighed at that point. That is around 65 percent throttle just to stay up, with almost nothing held back, and the datasheet’s continuous ratings are quoted per ten seconds rather than sustained.

  6. Resize the lift system and re-fly

    The lift units went to QM5010 380 KV on 15 inch props. Same KV, so the change buys disc area rather than tip speed, and the larger motor is there to drive it. On the manufacturer’s bench figures that puts the four motors at about 10.8 kg of static thrust against 5.5 kg all-up, near 2:1, with hover sitting around 60 percent throttle instead of 65 with nothing above it. The tractor motor was left alone, since the fault was in the lift axis only.

What the logs looked like

Why the site matters

Zaria sits at 670 m, and manufacturer thrust tables are not measured in that air. A propulsion sum that takes a datasheet figure at face value is optimistic before any other error is added, which is part of why a number that looked like 2:1 on paper did not behave like 2:1 in the air.

Prediction against measurement

With the lift system resized, the propulsion analysis was checked against what the aircraft learned for itself in flight. ArduPilot converges on a hover throttle and persists it, so the aircraft ends up holding its own measurement of the thrust it needs, arrived at independently of the sums that sized it.

MeasuredQ_M_THST_HOVER = 0.5496, the converged value from the aircraft’s own parameter file, giving a thrust-to-weight of 1.82:1 at 5.5 kg all-up
The fault was lift authority, not control gains. Correcting it meant a change of motor and propeller on the physical airframe.

The value in this is not the fix. It is that the aircraft was telling the truth in its logs the whole time, the sums were wrong because an assumption underneath them was wrong, and once that assumption was corrected the analysis predicted what the aircraft would go on to learn for itself.

04Perception

Indices computed in the air, not on the ground

The aircraft runs a GLI vegetation-index module and YOLOv8n inference on a Raspberry Pi 3B, through ONNX, against imagery from a SIYI A8 Mini. The reason is bandwidth. A survey aircraft working over the Sahel cannot count on a link that will carry raw imagery home, so the useful product has to be formed onboard and the link has to carry the result instead of the input. That constraint is the subject of the paper below.

The pipeline on the bench

Before it goes up, the pipeline is exercised on the bench against a live feed. Two things run at once: detection, with boxes and confidences drawn on the frame, and the vegetation index, rendered beside the raw view so the two can be compared directly. Hold something green in front of the camera and it is the only thing that lights up in the index view while the rest of the scene falls away, which is exactly the behaviour the index has to have before it is worth flying.

Bench run of the perception stack against a live feed, with the aircraft on the table beside it.

Perception-driven replanning, in progress

The work I am on now closes the loop: the aircraft triggers its own re-survey of a region from what it senses in flight, rather than flying a plan fixed before takeoff. This is where the project meets the control and autonomy questions I want to work on at doctoral level.

A printed hand that mirrors yours

A camera watches a human hand and a 3D-printed hand copies it, live. No gloves, no markers, no controller. Hold up your hand and the machine holds up its own.

Side by side

The printed hand is on the left, mine on the right. Fingers extend and curl to match as the gesture changes, driven from the camera feed alone.

Watching it next to the hand it is copying is the only honest way to show this working, because the interesting part is not that the fingers move, it is whether they move to the right positions.

01How it works

From camera to tendon

A MediaPipe pipeline extracts hand landmarks from a live camera feed, and those landmarks are mapped to joint targets. The targets drive tendon-driven fingers through MG996R servos on a PCA9685 driver, with an ESP32-CAM supplying the capture stream. The mechanism is the InMoov open platform, printed and assembled here.

02Edge port

Off the laptop, onto the robot

The pipeline first ran on a laptop with the robot as a peripheral. I then ported the control path to run standalone on a Raspberry Pi 4B and an Orange Pi, so the system is self-contained rather than tethered to a workstation.

That is the same constraint as the aircraft, arrived at from a different direction. Inference has to happen where the machine is, because a robot that needs a laptop nearby is not a robot, it is a peripheral.

Team leadShell Eco-marathon2025

An autonomous driving stack, and the team that built it

A ROS 2 stack that plans a route, watches the road ahead with LiDAR and steers a car through 14 goals in CARLA. I led Team ABU Zaria into the Shell Eco-marathon Autonomous Programming Competition 2025.

01The stack

Planning, perception, control

Four processes. Three are ours, and the fourth is the bridge the competition provides between ROS 2 and the simulator. The bridge publishes odometry, speed, a VLP-16 LiDAR and a depth camera, and takes throttle, brake, steering, gear and handbrake commands back. Each of our nodes answers one question.

planning_nodeWhere should we go? Routes to the current goal over CARLA’s road network and publishes the path. 10 Hz
perception_nodeIs anything in the way? Publishes the distance to the nearest obstacle ahead, and an alert flag
control_nodeHow do we drive there? Pure-pursuit steering and PID speed control, turned into bridge commands. 20 Hz

One connection is odd. The planning node opens its own CARLA client, separate from the bridge, because the road network it plans over is not published on any ROS topic. That makes it the only node that talks to both worlds, and the only one that has to convert coordinates between ROS’s frame and CARLA’s.

The pieces that carry the maths live outside ROS: the route graph and A* search, the pure-pursuit law, and the frame conversion are small plain-Python modules, so they can be tested without a simulator running.

02Planning

Two problems, solved in two different places

Getting a vehicle around a set of goals is really two questions, and they are easy to run together. Which order should the goals be visited in, and how does the car get from one goal to the next. They are solved at different levels here, and only one of them runs live.

A*How to drive between two consecutive goals. Live, on every replan, over CARLA’s road-network graph
TSPWhich order to visit the goals in. Precomputed offline, baked into the waypoint file, not solved at runtime

A*, in the loop

Route search runs inside the global route planner against CARLA’s road-network graph, sampled at 2 m, and returns the waypoint chain the controller then follows. The search is my own implementation on the standard library rather than a networkx dependency, which keeps the planner free of a heavyweight graph package for one function.

For each goal the node converts the car’s odometry into CARLA’s frame, traces the route, converts the waypoints back and publishes them as the path. A route with fewer than two points, or one that starts more than 25 m from the car, is rejected and retried rather than handed to the controller. Within 3 m of a goal the node moves on to the next one and replans, and after the fourteenth it publishes that the mission is complete.

The tour, computed once

The visiting order over the 14 goals is a 2-opt tour computed offline, using Manhattan distance as a proxy for Town01’s street grid. That gives a tour of about 1795 m against roughly 1975 m for a naive nearest-neighbour order, so about 9 percent shorter. The result is written into the waypoint file, and the planning node walks that list in order.

Worth being exact about this: the node is named for the TSP but does not solve one. It reads a precomputed order. Naming a variable after the problem it once solved is how a system quietly starts lying about itself, and it is worth saying out loud rather than letting a reader infer a runtime solver that is not there.

Where that falls down

Manhattan distance does not know about one-way streets or actual road topology. Two goals a hundred metres apart in a straight line can be six hundred metres apart by road if they sit on opposite sides of a divided street, and a tour optimised on the straight-line proxy cannot see that. The correct version builds the cost matrix from real routed distances between every pair of goals, using the same route tracing the planner already has, and solves the tour on that. It runs once at startup, and it is where the distance and energy scores actually live.

Making runs comparable

CARLA is only useful as a test bench if two runs of the same controller produce comparable numbers, and the early standalone test script was set up around that. The traffic manager runs in synchronous mode, so the simulation steps in lockstep with the client rather than drifting with whatever the machine is doing. Every traffic light in the world is forced green for the duration of a run. That second one is not a shortcut, it is removing a variable: a red light that catches one run and not the next contaminates the comparison with something that has nothing to do with the controller under test. Runs are timed over a fixed 120 second window, with progress and speed logged throughout.

03Driving

Seeing the road, then following the path

Perception: range, not recognition

The perception node answers a narrow question: how far away is the nearest thing in front of the car. It keeps LiDAR returns inside a 30° forward cone, drops anything closer than 3.5 m, because those returns are the car’s own bodywork, and publishes the nearest range that survives. The depth camera is the fallback when LiDAR is not available. There is an optional height window, off by default.

Steering: pure pursuit

The controller finds the closest point on the path, walks forward to a lookahead point between 5 and 15 m away depending on speed, and steers for the heading error to that point. The resulting road-wheel angle is divided by full lock to normalise it, then negated, because the bridge expects −1 for left and +1 for right while the geometry is positive to the left.

Speed: PID under an obstacle ceiling

Cruise target is 8 m/s, capped by a ceiling set from the obstacle distance: full cruise beyond 6 m, ramping down to a stop at 3 m. The sign of the PID output chooses throttle or brake. Gear and handbrake are republished every cycle, so a bridge restart cannot leave the car sitting in neutral.

04Debugging

The bugs that did not throw

Most of the bugs were ordinary: an import the environment did not have, the wrong goal list, a launch file hardcoded to localhost, the car’s own body showing up as an obstacle, and three separate ways for obstacle handling to deadlock the car.

The two that took longest were the frame conversion and the steering sign, and they had the same shape. Both produced plausible numbers, raised no exception, and simply pointed the wrong way. Neither could be found by reading the code. They showed up only by comparing what the car did against what it should have done, which is the argument for keeping the conversion and the steering law in small modules that can be checked against known answers.

Lumina: retinal screening that fits in a hand and talks to a phone

Diabetic retinopathy is one of the leading causes of blindness, and it is preventable when it is caught. What is scarce is not the treatment. It is the screening.

01The problem

The bottleneck is the instrument, not the diagnosis

Fundus cameras are expensive, fixed and concentrated in specialist eye clinics. In most of the places where diabetes is rising fastest, that means a patient is never screened at all, and the first anyone knows of the disease is when sight is already going. Africa carries a rising diabetic burden and very little of the diagnostic equipment that goes with it.

Lumina attacks the access problem rather than the diagnostic one. It is built to be cheap enough and portable enough to put in the hands of an ordinary healthcare worker, and it interfaces with a smartphone rather than requiring a workstation of its own.

02The device

Imaging, then reading the image

On the optical side, an adjustable coloured lighting system improves retinal illumination and a focus adjustment lets the operator work across different patients rather than a narrow ideal case. Both exist because the device has to work in the hands of a non-specialist, in a room that was not built for ophthalmology.

On the analysis side, AI does two jobs. It lifts image quality when capture conditions are poor, which is the normal case outside a clinic, and it reads the resulting image for signs of diabetic retinopathy and grades severity, so the healthcare worker gets an answer and a next step during the visit rather than after a referral.

MEDIC-AID: a dispenser that knows whether the pill actually fell

Medication Electronic Dispenser with Integrated Caregiver Alerts and IoT Dashboard. Turning a motor is the easy half. The work is in confirming the dose left the machine, and telling someone when it did not.

Three hoppers, one collection tray, one enclosure. The animation runs through loading each stage and a dose arriving in the tray.

01The system

Three stages, one dose

A patient on several medications takes a different combination at different hours. MEDIC-AID holds three drugs in three independent hoppers, each with its own stepper and its own drop sensor, and assembles a dose out of them on a schedule. The default programme runs three doses a day at 08:00, 14:00 and 20:00, each specifying how many pills come from which stage, so a dose can be one from stage one and two from stage two and nothing from stage three.

When a dose is due, the unit alerts the patient for five seconds before anything moves, dispenses stage by stage, and reports the outcome. A stage set to zero pills is skipped rather than cycled.

02Dispensing

Closed loop, not open loop

The naive version of this machine turns the motor a fixed number of steps and assumes a pill came out. That assumption fails exactly when it matters, on an empty hopper or a jam, and it fails silently.

Instead the firmware rotates in two-step chunks and counts pills as they are detected falling, advancing only when the detected count reaches the target for that stage. Small chunks rather than a single long rotation keep the sensor polling responsive, so a pill is never missed while the motor is busy. Dispensing is therefore driven by what was observed, not by what was commanded.

Faults name their own cause

If a stage passes about twenty revolutions without reaching its target, the firmware stops and declares a fault. It also decides which fault: nothing detected at all is reported as an empty bottle, while some pills detected and then nothing is reported as a suspected jam. Those need different responses from a caregiver, so the machine distinguishes them rather than raising one generic error.

03Remote

Schedule, commands and a caregiver on the other end

The dispenser talks to a hosted backend over HTTPS, which is what a caregiver reaches through Telegram and through the dashboard. Three things cross that link.

The dose schedule is pulled from the backend and merged over the built-in defaults field by field, so a failed sync leaves the machine running its last known programme rather than losing its schedule. Commands are polled every two seconds: dispense a full dose, dispense a single stage, or refresh the schedule. Each command is answered with a result, including a refusal, since a command that arrives mid-dose is rejected as busy rather than allowed to interleave with one already running.

In the other direction the unit posts an event stream: dose started, stage dispensed with target against actual, dose completed, and fault with its reason. That stream is the record a caregiver sees, and it is what makes a missed dose visible on the day rather than at the next refill.

04Hardware

The board

The hardware design puts an ESP32-S2-WROVER against a DS3231M real-time clock. Each of the three bipolar steppers gets its own DRV8833 driver with its fault line brought back to the microcontroller. Pill detection is piezoelectric: a sensor on each chute picks up the impact of a pill passing, which is the event the counting logic above consumes.

Status: designed and simulated, hardware in progress. The firmware, the dispensing logic and the alerting path all run end to end in simulation; the board and the enclosure are being built.

05Simulation

Verified in simulation before hardware

The whole system was brought up in Wokwi first, with the full firmware including Wi-Fi and the backend calls, so the dispensing logic and the alerting path could be exercised before any board existed. Two substitutions were made deliberately and recorded rather than left to surprise anyone later: the simulator carries a DS1307 in place of the DS3231, an API difference noted for real deployment, and the piezoelectric drop sensors are stood in for by push buttons, which produce the same discrete detection event the counting logic consumes.

06Decisions

The choices, and why

Much of the engineering on this project sits in decisions rather than in code, so these are worth setting out plainly.

  1. Wokwi over Proteus for simulation

    Native ESP32 support, the ability to simulate Wi-Fi, and free browser-based access. The Wi-Fi point decided it, because the caregiver alert is the part of this system most worth testing before hardware and the part a circuit simulator cannot reach.

  2. A full ESP32 DevKit in simulation, not the ESP32-C3

    A GPIO budget decision. The C3 exposes around 11 usable GPIOs and the design needs around 19, so it was ruled out by counting pins against the design rather than by discovering it during a build. The hardware design went to an ESP32-S2-WROVER for the same reason.

  3. DS1307 standing in for the DS3231

    The simulator carries the DS1307, so it was used in its place with the API difference written down as a known adjustment for deployment, rather than left to surface as a bug later.

  4. Build all three stages directly

    Rather than proving one stage and scaling it. The risk in this machine is in the sequencing between stages and in what happens when one of them fails mid-dose, and a single-stage proof of concept would not have exposed either.

  5. Defer Telegram and Wi-Fi until the dispensing logic was verified

    Connectivity is the easiest thing to debug and the easiest thing to hide behind. Getting the mechanical logic right in simulation first meant that when an alert failed afterwards, the cause was the alert.
